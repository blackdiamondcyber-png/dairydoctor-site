Pregnancy testing

About BioPRYN® and pregnancy testing

What is BioPRYN?

BioPRYN is a blood pregnancy test for cattle, bison, goats and sheep, and has specific appeal for the commercial U.S. dairy industry because it delivers fast, accurate, safe and economical pregnancy diagnostic results.

What does the test name stand for?

The test's name is a partial acronym for "Pregnant Ruminant Yes/No." This specific test uses ELISA technology, which produces fast results.

How does BioPRYN detect pregnancies?

BioPRYN evaluates the blood, more specifically the serum or plasma, of ruminants for a protein called Pregnancy Specific Protein B (PSPB). PSPB is produced by the placenta, so pregnant animals will have the protein in their blood. This makes the test more accurate than earlier attempts at pregnancy diagnosis that evaluated blood or milk for progesterone or other hormones that can occur in normally cycling animals. The test uses enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) technology for processing, which contributes to its low cost and fast turnaround.

How early in a pregnancy can animals be tested with BioPRYN?

Adult cattle can be tested for pregnancy 28 or more days following insemination; virgin heifers can be tested 25 days or more after insemination. Goats and sheep can be tested from 30 days after breeding. Animals that are detected open can then be immediately returned to time-saving re-synchronization protocols using products such as Estroplan and Gonabreed.

How soon after calving can a cow be tested?

Cows should be at least 90 days since calving as well as 28 or more days from breeding, so the protein from the last pregnancy has cleared. For does and ewes that kidded or lambed recently, ask when you call.

What is the accuracy of BioPRYN?

The test is more than 99% accurate in detecting open cows (BioTracking's published figure), with less than 1% showing false-open (false-negative) results. Correct open detection is very important when giving Estroplan or other synchronizing drugs.

What does a "recheck" result mean?

BioPRYN reports each animal as pregnant, open, or recheck. A recheck means the reading fell between the two ranges: a pregnancy in trouble, an animal sampled too early, or a cow still too close to calving. Every plate is read by the lab's veterinarian, and a recheck comes with a note on when to sample her again.

What are the advantages of using blood testing versus other methods of pregnancy diagnosis?
  • Producers incur less cost using BioPRYN compared to rectal palpation by a veterinarian, and much less cost than ultrasound.
  • Blood testing allows producers to diagnose pregnancy at least 7 days sooner than rectal palpation, with no risk of damaging the fetus.
  • Cows diagnosed open can then be re-bred sooner, resulting in tighter calving intervals, more calves born per year, and higher lifetime milk production, because cows achieve peak milk more often.
  • The test is more convenient for dairies, because on-farm personnel can draw samples without locking up cows or waiting for the veterinarian to arrive, and then ship the blood samples.
  • Eliminating rectal palpation also frees up the veterinarian to concentrate on higher-level issues with the dairy's management team.
Can heifers at a custom grower be tested?

Yes. Heifers test from 25 days after breeding. The grower's crew draws the samples and ships them, and the report goes wherever you mark on the form. See heifer testing.

Do you test goats and sheep?

Yes. Goats and sheep test from the same 2cc blood draw as cattle, from 30 days after breeding, on the Small Ruminant Pregnancy Test Form. See goat and sheep testing.

Do you test bison?

Yes. The assay is validated in bison, at the same price as cattle, on the cattle and bison form.

Samples

Drawing and shipping samples

Do I need a veterinarian to draw the samples?

No. Your own crew can draw from the tail vein (goats and sheep: the jugular vein). See how to tail bleed a cow, which walks through it one step at a time.

What tubes should I use?

Red-top tubes, 3cc or 5cc, with at least 2cc of blood in each. We can ship tubes and needles with your first order; see the supplies list.

How should blood samples be prepared and shipped?

Dairy personnel draw blood samples from the tail vein, an easy-to-learn procedure; goats and sheep are drawn from the jugular vein. See how to tail bleed a cow. Blood samples of at least 2cc per animal should be collected in individual vacuum tubes and labeled with each animal's identification number. It is important to draw samples using individual, disposable needles to avoid cross-contamination between animals. Tubes containing blood samples should not be opened, and should be packed in a well-padded box to avoid breakage. They do not need to be packed in ice. Samples may stay in transit for several days without compromising the results of the test. The surest way to make the Wednesday cutoff is FedEx, UPS or U.S. Postal Service Priority Mail, shipped early in the week.

Do the samples need ice?

Pregnancy blood tubes do not. Ear notches and BVD blood samples do: refrigerate them, pack them with ice packs, and get them to the lab within 48 hours of collection.

What if a tube breaks in transit?

Call the lab. We will tell you which animals need a new sample.

Results

Results and payment

How long does it take to receive the test results?

Tests are run Monday through Thursday, and results are reported on Thursday. Samples that reach the lab by Wednesday go out in that week's Thursday report, faxed or emailed back to the dairy.

We do not rush the assay; accuracy is the priority here, not turnaround. Every result is read by the veterinarian who runs the lab.

How are results sent?

By email, fax or phone, whichever you mark on the form, on Thursday for samples that reach the lab by Wednesday.

How do I pay?

Include payment with your samples; checks are payable to ADDC. Pregnancy tests are $3.50 per head for cattle and bison and $7.00 for goats and sheep; BVD PI tests are $6.95 per animal. The amounts are printed on the submission form.
